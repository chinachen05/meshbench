Field note 09 / Materials

Fine mesh for tiny biting insects

Smaller openings can improve exclusion while affecting airflow and view.

Editorially prepared 15 November 2026

The decision you need to make

If small biting midges or similar insects pass through standard screening, a finer weave may help. What matters is the product’s opening size and intact installation, not only its marketing label. Finer mesh can change the amount of air and light passing through the opening.

What to inspect first

  • Check that insects are entering through the mesh rather than around door seals, window tracks or a torn corner.
  • Compare the supplier’s published mesh count or aperture information for standard and no-see-um products.
  • Observe whether the affected window is an important ventilation path before changing every panel.

A practical sequence

  1. Try a sample in the most affected opening and check visibility and airflow in real use.
  2. Measure the groove and choose spline appropriate for the fine mesh product.
  3. Inspect the entire perimeter after rescreening; tiny gaps defeat a finer central weave.
  4. Keep doors shut or screened and address standing-water sources as part of mosquito management.

Worked situation

A bedroom window has intact 18×16 insect mesh but very small insects appear indoors at dusk. Before replacing it, inspect the window-frame gap and nearby light attractants, then compare a fine-mesh sample.

Common wrong turn

Finer mesh is not a substitute for eliminating other entry points; neither is it proof of a mosquito-free room.

When to stop:

For persistent indoor biting insects, consider local pest identification and public-health guidance rather than relying on screen terminology alone.
